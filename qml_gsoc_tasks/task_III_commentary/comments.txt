What took me by surprise in relation to VQC was not the circuits but the gradient.
In deep learning, the autograd algorithm traverses the computation graph while applying the chain rule.
That's simple enough. However, the problem with a quantum circuit running on hardware or even just a simulator is that it is a black box.

This is where the parameter shift rule comes in. The idea is simple enough; for any rotation gate with rotation parameter θ,
the gradient will be precisely the difference between two forward computations: increase θ by π/2, decrease it by π/2, 
take the difference, divide by 2. There is no need for an approximation, and we get the exact derivative of the circuit's output 
with respect to that single parameter. Before implementing PennyLane's built-in parameter shift rule, 
I had hand-coded my own implementation for a custom autograd function, so the automation didn't seem too magical at all.
Of course, the price tag of such precision is not negligible either. With two forward evaluations per single parameter, 
a circuit with 36 parameters requires 72 circuit evaluations for each gradient evaluation step. Compare this to backpropagation, 
which requires only 2 forward evaluations irrespective of the number of parameters. Training quantum circuits takes time because of this, 
which makes efforts to maintain their small size well justified.
PennyLane is the software package that I am most familiar with. Why I like it is because in it, 
quantum circuits are nothing more than regular differentiable functions. Define a QNode, then wrap it with TorchLayer

One thing I figured out myself that wasn't in any tutorial: the activation function before your angle embedding matters a lot. 
I was using Tanh to squash inputs, which keeps them in (-1, 1). But AngleEmbedding treats these as rotation angles in radians. 
Tanh was restricting my circuit to a tiny slice of the Bloch sphere — roughly ±57 degrees out of 360. Removing the Tanh and clipping to [-π, π] directly improved 
my hybrid classifier accuracy noticeably. The interface between classical preprocessing and quantum encoding is underrated in most QML papers.

The area that I wish to improve on is quantum attention. Specifically, using quantum circuits as an alternative to the linear projections (W_Q, W_K, W_V) involved 
in computing the attention mechanism of a transformer. It involves encoding a token embedding as rotation angles, using it to perform parameterized gates on an 
entangled quantum state and then computing expectation values, the attention scores being computed in a classical manner using the results of those operations.

A working prototype of such an attention mechanism exists in my codebase as applied to the MNIST dataset. The question that I wish to investigate with regards
to the said prototype is whether the quantum attention mechanism achieves something that cannot be achieved with a classical linear transformation layer 
of similar parameter size. MNIST is unlikely to have such questions arise since the dataset is too neat to be considered challenging. The dataset of jets in particle physics,
however, where jets consist of multi-particle substructure, likely does.

One thing that I have to figure out is noise. The only things that I worked on so far were noiseless simulators. In real devices, however, depolarisation noise deteriorates gradients,
causing instability of training process. I have read about two approaches to deal with this – zero-noise extrapolation and probabilistic error correction.
However, I never tried implementing any of them. If QVIT can only be trained in simulators, my project does not have much practical significance.
